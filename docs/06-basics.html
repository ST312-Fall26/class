P(A|B) = \(\frac{P(A \text{and} B)}{P(B)}\)

Out of the people in B, how many are also in A?

The denominator is P(B) because once we’re told B happened, we only care about the B group.

Probability Rules

Independence:

\(P(A|B) = P(A)\)

Recall normally: \(P(A \text{and} B)\) = P(A|B)*P(B)

Now: \(P(A \text{and} B)\) = P(A) * P(B)

Probability Rules

Bayes Rule: \(P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A)P(A)}{P(B)}\)

Suppose:

A - having the disease

\(A^c\) - not having the disease

B - positive test

\(B^c\) - negative test

Bayes

\(P(A)\) - Starting point

Only __% of people have the disease before looking at the test

\(P(B|A)\) - How good is the evidence?

If someone actually has the disease, there’s a ___% chance they’ll test positive (likelihood)

\(P(B)\) - how common is the evidence

___% of everyone tests positive.

Proof

\[ P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A \mid B)P(B) \] \[\text{And probability can be re-written as so!}\]

\[ P(A \text{ and } B) = P(B \mid A)P(A) \]

\[\text{plug in what P(A and B) is from above}\]

\[ P(A \mid B)P(B) = P(B \mid A)P(A) \]

\[\text{divide by B}\]

\[ P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A)P(A)}{P(B)} \]

P(O)

If I want the probability of O, I want the probability when O and E occur, and the probability when O and \(E^c\) occur….

Let’s think back to this rule: P(A|B) = \(\frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}\)

P(O|E) = \(\frac{P(O \cap E)}{P(E)}\)

How can we rewrite this?

P(O)

\(P(O | E) = \frac{P(O \cap E)}{P(E)}\)

\(P(O\cap E)=P(O\mid E)P(E)\)

and

\(P(O| E^c) = \frac{P(O \cap E^c)}{P(E^c)}\)

\(P(O\cap E^c)=P(O\mid E^c)P(E^c)\)

and we can add them together to get \(P(O)\)!

Probability Rule

\(P(A) = P(A and B) + P(A and B^c)\)

What is this called?

Probability Rule

\(P(A) = P(A and B) + P(A and B^c)\)

Law of total probability!

Think back

\(P(E)=0.60\)

\(P(E^c)=0.40\)

\(P(O\mid E)=0.90\)

\(P(O\mid E^c)=0.60\)

and now we can answer the question!

\[ P(E \mid O) = \frac{P(O \mid E)P(E)}{P(O)} \]

Stratified random sample

To select a stratified random sample, first divide the population into groups of similar individuals, called strata. Then choose a separate SRS in each stratum and combine these SRSs to form the full sample.

alpha

Remind me, what is \(\alpha\) when we conduct a hypothesis test?

alpha

Type I error rate - probility of rejecting the null hypothesis when the null hypothesis is true

One reason for the common use of \(\alpha = 0.05\) is the great influence of Sir R. A. Fisher, the inventor of formal statistical methods for analyzing experimental data. Here is his opinion on choosing a level of significance:

“A scientific fact should be regarded as experimentally established only if a properly designed experiment rarely fails to give this level of significance.”

alpha

Recently, certain scientific communities, which have historically used \(\alpha = 0.05\), have proposed lowering the threshold to \(\alpha = 0.05\) to \(\alpha = 0.005\). This is in response to the lack of reproducibility of study results. By lowering the threshold, it is argued that an initial discovery has a greater chance of being confirmed in follow-up studies.

Abuse of alpha

Here’s a dirty little science secret: If you measure a large number of things about a small number of people, you are almost guaranteed to get a “statistically significant” result. Our study included 18 different measurements—weight, cholesterol, sodium, blood protein levels, sleep quality, well-being, etc.—from 15 people. (One subject was dropped.) That study design is a recipe for false positives.

Practical significance vs statistical significance

Practical significance - Whether the size of an effect is large enough to matter in the real world.

Statistical significance - Whether the observed effect is unlikely to have occurred by random chance, based on a chosen significance level (such as \(\alpha=0.05)\)
